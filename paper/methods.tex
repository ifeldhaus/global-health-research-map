\section{Methods}

\subsection{Corpus}

The corpus is the set of works indexed by OpenAlex~\cite{openalex} whose
primary publication venue is one of eleven journals that publish global health
research, with a publication year from 2010 through 2026. The eleven journals
and their ISSNs are listed in Table~\ref{tab:journals}. The corpus was
retrieved as a single snapshot in July 2026; 2026 is therefore a partial
publication year, and per-year analyses either exclude it or mark it as
incomplete. Citation counts and funding records reflect the state of OpenAlex
at the snapshot date.

A journal-defined boundary was used rather than a keyword or concept-tag
search. Re-running the retrieval against the same journal list and date window
returns the same corpus, apart from subsequent OpenAlex index updates. The
boundary excludes global health research published in general medical journals;
this is stated as a limitation. The retrieval returned 33{,}964 works.

\subsection{Abstract availability}

OpenAlex stores abstracts as inverted indexes, which were reconstructed to
plain text at retrieval. Of the 33{,}964 works, 4{,}516 (13.3\%) lacked a
usable abstract: 2{,}814 had no abstract, 1{,}592 carried journal boilerplate
in place of an abstract, and 110 had an abstract of 50 characters or fewer.
These works were labeled by the reason their abstract was unusable and were
excluded from classification rather than treated as missing at random. The
remaining 29{,}448 works (86.7\%) carry all three classification labels
described below. Because the unusable-abstract works are concentrated in
specific journals, the topic, methods, and study-country analyses are
understood to under-represent those journals; analyses that do not depend on
the abstract (publication counts, authorship, funding, institutions) use the
full corpus.

\subsection{Classification}

Each work with a usable abstract was classified in three passes over its title
and abstract (the abstract truncated to 300 words), using the large language
model Claude Opus 4.8 (Anthropic). The three passes and their outputs were:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topic.} One of 15 primary categories (labeled A--O) and one of
  143 subtopics, with a self-reported confidence level, and an optional
  secondary category and subtopic (described below).
  \item \textbf{Methods.} One of 18 study-design types (labeled M01--M18), with
  a self-reported confidence level.
  \item \textbf{Study country.} The country or countries in which the research
  was conducted, as ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 codes (up to six, for multi-country
  studies), or \texttt{GLOBAL} for region-wide or global work naming no
  specific country, or \texttt{UNKNOWN}, with a self-reported confidence level.
\end{itemize}

The classification prompts were generated from the taxonomy definition files so
that the taxonomy is the single source of each label set. Model responses were
parsed and checked against the taxonomy; responses that were malformed or fell
outside the taxonomy were assigned the uncategorized values
(\texttt{Z} for topic, \texttt{M18} for methods, \texttt{UNKNOWN} for study
country) rather than admitted as data. This produced 88{,}344 labels
(29{,}448 works $\times$ 3 passes). Study country denotes the location of the
study population or data collection, not author affiliation; the prompt
instructed the model not to infer it from affiliations. Across the classified
works, the uncategorized values were assigned to 5.2\% of topic labels, 3.8\%
of methods labels, and 4.6\% of study-country labels; \texttt{GLOBAL} was
assigned to 35.5\% of study-country labels, reflecting the corpus's share of
systematic reviews, multi-country studies, and global-policy writing.

The secondary topic category is a descriptive enrichment layer: works that
address a co-equal second topic carry a second category and subtopic, covering
approximately 21\% of the corpus. It is reported separately from the primary
topic and is not used in the primary analyses.

\subsection{Validation}

Classification accuracy was assessed against 345 papers hand-labeled by a
single human coder who did not see the model's labels, drawn from across the
eleven journals (199 papers in a first round and 146 in a second; papers with
no genuine abstract, such as front-matter and corrections, were skipped). The
labels evaluated were the first-pass classifier output; no validation paper was
used to develop the classifier, so all 345 papers are a held-out test set.
Agreement was measured with Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960} and interpreted
using the Landis and Koch descriptors~\cite{landis1977}. Agreement was
substantial for the primary topic category ($\kappa = 0.66$; 69.3\% raw
agreement) and for methods ($\kappa = 0.67$; 70.6\% agreement), moderate for
the 143-way topic subtopic ($\kappa = 0.54$), and almost perfect for study
country ($\kappa = 0.93$; 93.9\% exact-set agreement, computed on the primary
country). Exact-set agreement for the topic labels including the optional
secondary category was 0.54. These figures are reported in full in the
repository's validation report.

\subsection{Author gender}

The likely gender of the first and last authors was inferred from their given
names using the Genderize.io service~\cite{genderize}, following two
adjustments recommended by Santamar\'ia and Mihaljevi\'c~\cite{santamaria2018}:
diacritics were removed, and for compound given names the first component was
used. Names returned below a probability of 0.6, single initials, and names for
which no gender could be determined were recorded as unknown. The inference is
probabilistic and binary and is less accurate for non-Western names; results
are reported only at the level of the corpus, never for individual authors.

\subsection{Funding}

Funding was taken from the grant records that OpenAlex stores for each work.
Each acknowledged funder was matched to a curated canonical funder list by its
OpenAlex funder identifier, with name aliases as a fallback, so that variant
names for the same funder were combined. The completeness of funder metadata
varies over time and is weaker before 2015; it is therefore treated as a
measured quantity rather than assumed complete, and unfunded rates are read as
lower bounds.

\subsection{Disease burden}

Disease burden was taken from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation
Global Burden of Disease 2023 results~\cite{gbd2023}, using
disability-adjusted life years and deaths (counts, all ages, both sexes). Each
topic category was mapped to its corresponding GBD cause groups through a
hand-built crosswalk. Comparisons of research attention to disease burden use
the pooled World Bank low- and middle-income aggregate (the low, lower-middle,
and upper-middle income groups combined), because the corpus concerns global
health research directed largely at those settings. Burden values are fixed at
the GBD 2023 release regardless of the corpus date window.

\subsection{Analytic definitions and derived measures}

\paragraph{Analytic subset.}
Analyses of topics, methods, geography, funding, and institutions were
restricted to research articles: works classified as narrative review (M14),
commentary, editorial, or perspective (M15), or undeterminable design (M18)
were excluded, as were works with an uncategorized topic (Z). The corpus
overview reports all works, and a separate analysis profiles the commentary and
editorial works on their own.

\paragraph{Leadership.}
An institution or country was credited with a work when one of its researchers
was the first or last author, the positions that conventionally denote study
leadership in health research. Middle-author positions were not counted toward
leadership.

\paragraph{Institution identity.}
Institutions were identified by their OpenAlex institution identifier rather
than by display name, because one display name (for example, ``Ministry of
Health'') is shared by many distinct national bodies. Where a name was shared
across multiple identifiers, the country was appended so that each national
entity is counted separately.

\paragraph{Income groups.}
Institutions and study countries were grouped by the World Bank income
classification for the 2024 fiscal year~\cite{worldbank2024}. Analyses
contrast high-income with combined low- and middle-income countries; where
noted, the four World Bank tiers (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, high income)
are shown separately.

\paragraph{Concentration.}
Concentration of funding and of institutional production was summarized with
the Gini coefficient and with the share held by the most productive decile and
percentile.

\paragraph{Research leadership by geography.}
For research about a single country, leadership was classified from author
affiliations relative to that country: local-led (the last author is affiliated
with the study country), collaborative (an author, but not the last, is
affiliated with the study country), or externally led, the last case
corresponding to research conducted in a country with no author based there.
Studies spanning multiple countries were excluded from this classification.

\paragraph{Attention relative to burden.}
For each topic, its share of publications was compared with its share of low-
and middle-income disease burden. The two shares measure different quantities;
their comparison is descriptive and is not interpreted as a causal or normative
target.

\paragraph{Evidence strength.}
The 18 study-design types were grouped into three tiers for analysis:
experimental and synthesis designs, observational and analytic designs, and
descriptive and exploratory designs. The grouping is given in the taxonomy
files.

\paragraph{Citation influence.}
Influence was measured with the OpenAlex cited-by count at the snapshot date.
Because citations accumulate over time, recent works are cited less than older
works of equal influence, and citation-based comparisons are read with that in
mind.

\paragraph{Collaboration.}
Collaboration breadth was measured as the number of distinct partner
institutions (by OpenAlex identifier) with which an institution shared
authorship on at least one work.

\subsection{Reproducibility and software}

The retrieval, classification, enrichment, and validation steps are implemented
as numbered scripts; the taxonomies, the canonical funder list, the disease
burden crosswalk, the validation labels, and the agreement script are included
in the repository, and the corpus is rebuildable from OpenAlex. Analyses were
materialized into summary tables and are presented through an interactive
dashboard covering the corpus overview, the funding, geographic, topic,
methods, and institution analyses, and the abstract-availability report. Data
were held and queried in DuckDB~\cite{duckdb}; the dashboard was built with
Streamlit~\cite{streamlit}. The code, data, and dashboard are available at
\url{https://github.com/ifeldhaus/global-health-research-map}.
